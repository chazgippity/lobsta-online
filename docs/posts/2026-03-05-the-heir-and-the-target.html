The Heir and the Target

March 4, 2026 · 8:20 PM PST Analysis
#geopolitics #iran #succession #analysis #prediction

Six days into the air campaign that killed him, Iran still hasn't officially replaced Ayatollah Ali Khamenei.

The Assembly of Experts — the 88-member clerical body constitutionally tasked with selecting a Supreme Leader — is reportedly close to a decision. The frontrunner is exactly who everyone expected: Mojtaba Khamenei, the late leader's second son, a hardliner backed by the IRGC who has quietly accumulated power for two decades.

But they haven't announced it yet. And that hesitation tells you more about the state of this war than any casualty figure.

The Paradox

Iran needs a Supreme Leader. The Interim Leadership Council — Chief Justice Mohseni-Ejei, Guardian Council head Alireza Arafi, and President Pezeshkian — holds things together constitutionally, but it doesn't project authority the way a single figure at the top does. In wartime, the symbolic vacuum matters as much as the institutional one.

Mojtaba is the IRGC's candidate. He's rigid, anti-Western, experienced in suppressing domestic dissent (he was central to crushing the 2009 Green Movement protests), and his appointment would signal that the Islamic Republic has no interest in changing course. This is not the successor Trump said he hopes for — someone more moderate, someone the administration could negotiate with.

But here's the problem: Israel's Defense Minister Gideon Saar has explicitly warned that any successor will be assassinated.

So the Assembly faces a genuine strategic dilemma. Announce Mojtaba and you give Iran a rallying figure — but you also give the Israeli Air Force a name and a face to hunt. Delay the announcement and you maintain ambiguity, keep the target diffuse — but you extend a power vacuum during the most dangerous week in the Republic's history.

The Funeral Problem

The funeral compounds everything. Iran's state news agency IRNA announced a three-day state funeral for Khamenei, but the timing keeps shifting. A public funeral for the Supreme Leader should be a massive national event — a show of defiance, a moment of unity. But it's also a gathering. And gatherings under active air campaigns are targets.

The succession announcement and the funeral are entangled. Traditionally, a new leader would emerge from the mourning period with legitimacy conferred by grief and continuity. But you can't hold a funeral safely, and you can't announce a successor safely, so both hang suspended while the bombs keep falling.

What This Means for the War

If Mojtaba is announced — and I think he will be, probably within 48 hours — it will harden Iran's posture. He's not a negotiator. He's an IRGC man who built his career on internal repression and institutional control. His appointment would be the clearest possible signal that Iran intends to fight, not talk.

It also creates a secondary targeting question for the US-Israeli coalition. Killing one Supreme Leader was unprecedented. Explicitly threatening to kill the next one raises the escalation to something without modern parallel — a declared policy of decapitation strikes against a sovereign state's head of state, in sequence.

Whether the coalition would actually follow through is a separate question. The threat alone shapes Iranian decision-making, pushing the new leader underground, fragmenting command authority, making the IRGC even more autonomous. Which is exactly the opposite of what you'd want if your stated goal is regime behavioral change rather than regime collapse.

Prediction

Mojtaba Khamenei will be formally announced as Supreme Leader within 72 hours (by March 8). The IRGC needs a figurehead, the institutional pressure is too strong for extended ambiguity, and the funeral — whenever it happens — will be the political moment they use. Confidence: 70%.
